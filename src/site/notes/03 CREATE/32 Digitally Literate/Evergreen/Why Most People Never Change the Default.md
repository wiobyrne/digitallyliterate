# Why Most People Never Change the Default

The default effect is the tendency for people to stay with whatever option is already selected. A default is the choice a system makes for you when you make no choice yourself, and most of the time, it is the choice that sticks.

The common question is, "Why don't people change their settings?" The more useful one is, "Who picked the setting they were given?" Defaults show up everywhere: the privacy settings on a new phone, the sharing options in a school app, the retirement plan at a new job.

## Defaults stick for ordinary reasons

Changing a default takes time, attention, and some sense of what the options mean. Most people open a tool to get something done, and the settings screen is a detour. A default also looks like a recommendation. If the company or the school chose it, many people assume it is the sensible choice.

Think of a form at a doctor's office with a box already checked. Leaving it alone feels like agreeing with the people who designed the form, and it is the fastest way back to the waiting room.

Ask: what would someone have to know, and how long would it take, to choose differently?

## The default is the decision for most people

Because so few people change defaults, whoever sets the default is making the decision for nearly everyone. Organ donation is the best-known example. Johnson and Goldstein (2003) compared European countries and found that those that sign people up automatically, with a way to opt out, count far more registered donors than countries that ask people to opt in.

Retirement savings shows the same pattern. When a large U.S. company began enrolling new employees in its 401(k) plan automatically, participation rose sharply, and many workers kept the default contribution rate and investment fund (Madrian & Shea, 2001). The people and their values were the same in each case. The form was different.

Ask: if nobody touches this setting, what happens to them?

## In technology, defaults are policy

The same pattern runs through digital tools. A messaging app that encrypts only when you turn it on protects the few people who know to look. A school platform that shares student data with outside companies unless a parent objects is, in practice, a platform that shares student data. A new account that starts out public will stay public for most of the people who open one.

This is why the people who build and buy technology carry more responsibility than the people who use it. A district choosing a vendor, or a company shipping an update, is setting the terms for everyone downstream.

Ask: who chose this default, and what did they gain from it?

## Start close to home

Pick one app you use every day and open its settings once.

- Which choices were made for you when you signed up?
- Which of them would you have chosen yourself?
- If you changed something, does it stay changed after the next update?

## Limits and open questions

Defaults are powerful, but they are not the whole story. Actual organ donation also depends on hospitals, families, and medical systems, so signing up more donors does not turn into the same number of transplants. Defaults can also steer people toward choices that serve the company more than the person, which is why who sets them matters as much as what they are.

Open questions:

- Should a protective default ever be hard to change?
- Who is accountable when a harmful default affects millions of people who never saw it?
