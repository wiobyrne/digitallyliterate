# What Can People Do About AI?

Most people do not get to decide what a frontier lab (one of the companies building the most advanced AI models) trains next. That does not mean they have no role in how AI enters their lives.

The common question is, “Can I personally stop AI?” The more useful one is, “Where are decisions about AI being made around me, and what would it take for those decisions to be open to question?” AI becomes real in a school, workplace, public agency, community organization, or family long before it becomes an abstract debate about the future of humanity.

## 1. Who is making the decision?

Every AI system arrives through a decision: a district buys a product, a workplace changes a policy, an app turns on a feature, or a public agency adopts a vendor. Start there.

Think of a new traffic light at a school crossing. Someone asked for it, someone paid for it, and someone decided where it would go. Nobody believes it simply appeared. “The technology is here” deserves the same skepticism. It can hide choices about money, staffing, risk, and whose work will change.

Ask: Who made this decision, what problem were they trying to solve, and who was included before it was made?

## 2. What can the system actually reach?

An AI system is more than a chat box. It may be connected to student records, documents, email, cloud storage, cameras, calendars, accounts, or other people’s work. Its real power comes from those connections.

When you give a house sitter a key, how trustworthy they seem is only the start. You also want to know which doors the key opens, whether it also opens the filing cabinet, and whether you get it back when they leave. The same goes for an AI system. The answer should be specific enough that a person can understand the consequences of saying yes.

Ask: What information can the system see, what actions can it take, and what access does it keep after a task is complete?

## 3. What is the evidence that this use is worth it?

Promises of efficiency, personalization, safety, or innovation are not evidence on their own. A meaningful case for using an AI system should explain what it improves, for whom, compared with what alternative, and at what cost.

A new medicine has to show that it works, for the people who will take it, better than what already exists, with its side effects known. A sales pitch would never get it onto a pharmacy shelf. A vendor demonstration is not the same thing as an accountable evaluation either.

Ask: What does the evidence show, what has not been tested, and do the people who will live with the system recognize the claimed benefit?

## 4. Who can challenge the system, and what happens when it fails?

The final question is about power. Can a student, family, employee, or resident ask how a decision was made? Can they correct an error, opt out, appeal a result, or get a person involved? Can the organization pause or remove the tool if the harms outweigh the benefits?

When a bank charges you a fee by mistake, you can call, dispute it, and reach a person who can reverse it. If no one can do that for an AI system, it is asking for trust without offering accountability. Leaving that gap open is a governance choice.

Ask: If this system gets something wrong about me, who do I go to, and what can they actually change?

## Start close to home

Try the questions on one real case. Say your child’s school announces an AI tutor this fall:

- Who chose it, and were teachers or families asked first?
- Does it see grades and messages, or only what students type into it?
- What did the pilot show, and for which students?
- If it tells your child something wrong, who do you contact?

## Limits and open questions

These questions will not resolve every disagreement about AI. They can make the disagreement visible, locate responsibility, and give people a way to ask for better conditions before a system becomes too ordinary to question.

They work best where an institution has to answer: a school board, an employer with a policy process, a public agency. They have less purchase on consumer apps that change features overnight, or on vendors with no local accountability.

Open questions:

- What should people do when nobody in the room can answer these questions?
- Who should be asking them on behalf of people who are never in the room?
