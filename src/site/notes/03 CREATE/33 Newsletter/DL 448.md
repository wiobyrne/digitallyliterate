*Issue 448 · Published September 16, 2026 · Updated September 19, 2026*

Digitally Literate is a weekly letter about the forces shaping how we read, write, learn, and live in digital spaces. [Subscribe](https://digitallyliterate.net/subscribe/) to get each issue by email.

Jacob Coxon's resignation from Anthropic was at the top of my list to write about this week. Then, serendipitously, a local news station reached out asking me to talk about the exact same story on camera.

The pitch was “I’m focusing my story on what people should be aware of with all the reports that are saying AI could kill all humans by 2030. Like what should people know, should people be worried, etc.”

Prepping to say something coherent to a general audience in a five-minute segment is a pretty effective way to find out what in a story you actually understand versus what you’ve just been absorbing secondhand. This issue is the sorting-out process, written down.

## The warning, before anyone spun it

On September 8, Jacob Coxon resigned from Anthropic. He’d spent three years doing pre-training research at both OpenAI and Anthropic before that. At 8:04 PM that evening, he posted [a seven-part thread on X](https://x.com/hilbertspaess/status/2097476196791709843). It has since crossed 172 million views. I’ve presented the thread below with some comments.

> “I resigned from Anthropic today. I spent the last three years doing pretraining research at both OpenAI and Anthropic. Neither company is acting responsibly. They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives. More thoughts below.”

It’s worth slowing down at the term *self-improving superintelligence*. Using one generation of AI to help build or evaluate the next is one thing. Coxon is warning about a stronger possibility: systems that exceed human capabilities and can accelerate their own further improvement. The question is how much of that process people can still understand and control. We shouldn’t treat assistance with AI development as proof that the entire process has become autonomous.

> “Do not underestimate the power of this technology. These will soon be superhuman systems that can hack anything, revolutionize any field overnight, and acquire real power and resources. We have all witnessed the progress in each of these domains, and progress is not slowing.”

Coxon highlights three specific claims: offensive hacking capability, research-grade acceleration, and autonomous resource acquisition. Given Coxon’s insider status, we need to consider his warnings rather than general anxiety about AI.

> “The people building AI earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade. This is not a marketing stunt. If anything, many executives and senior researchers will couch their phrasing in the press to sound sensible — but I hear the same people express fear privately. No other human activity poses this level of danger.”

Coxon is doing two things in this tweet. First, he’s leaning on his insider status to share what conversations are like in the companies where these models are built. He differentiates between what insiders say to the press and public, and what they say privately to each other. He also highlights the “this is not a marketing stunt” point to anticipate future arguments.

One extraordinary claim buried in there, too: “No other human activity poses this level of danger.” In a *normal world,* we’d have public dialogue about things that *could kill us all.* My initial takeaway is that we need the dialogue and more than one person’s private conversations to support it.

> “A common response is ‘if they truly believe this, why are they still building it?’ At OpenAI, many have not deeply internalized the civilizational stakes. At Anthropic, the stakes are well-understood, but they are locked in a race to get there first — they believe no one else will act responsibly, so they must do it themselves, despite the risk.”

In this tweet, I see Coxon addressing the objection every reader will have: why are you building something you know will kill everyone? He presents two different diagnoses, one per company. Again, this is Coxon’s read on the two companies’ internal cultures.

- OpenAI hasn’t fully internalized the stakes. Possibly a culture or knowledge problem. Seems he thinks they don’t take the risk as seriously as he thinks they should.
- Anthropic does understand the stakes, but is trapped in a race as we’re caught in this logic that we need to move fast and get there first so we do it safely. Seems like a coordination problem, as a cautious actor accelerates because they can’t trust anyone else to hold back, so caution alone doesn’t produce restraint.

> “Accepting this race and entering the ‘endgame’ is a hubristic gamble that should not be launched from a private company’s Slack. Attempting to speedrun alignment should require extraordinary confidence that there are no better trajectories available.”

This is the line that matters most to me. A decision at this scale is being made inside private chat conversations at a handful of companies, by a small group of people, with no public input and no accountability. We need public dialogue about this, not some Slack channel texts about the end of the world.

> “I am optimistic about the potential for coordination. Warning shots like the Hugging Face attack have made pacing agreements between U.S. labs more viable. I don’t feel like we’re on track to prevent a global race, which may require costly actions such as a temporary ban on improving model capabilities.”

Readers of this newsletter know that we covered the Hugging Face incident in depth in [[03 CREATE/33 Newsletter/DL 441\|DL 441]] and [[03 CREATE/33 Newsletter/DL 442\|DL 442]]. With this tweet thread, Coxon is adding insight into how people at the companies view these events. His use of the phrase *warning shot* describes an incident serious enough to demonstrate a danger, but short of the catastrophe he fears. Highlighting U.S.-based labs is interesting, as labs agreeing to pace themselves doesn’t touch the international competitive pressure that is supposedly driving the race in the first place. He ends with a concrete ask (a temporary ban on improving model capabilities), not just “be careful.”

> “If you are a lab researcher, I urge you to consider what the next few years will actually feel like. Do you want to kick off a superintelligent RL run without a rigorous understanding of its mind? Should you put your head down because ‘it’s happening anyway’ — or take this moment to call for different conditions?”

In the closing tweet, he pivots from the general public to a specific audience. He stops addressing the over 172 million people who saw this thread and turns to the ending for the people working at the frontier labs.

He describes a *superintelligent RL* run without a rigorous understanding of its mind. The RL run is a technical term; it refers to the reinforcement-learning training process that shapes a model’s behavior. His point is that labs are running this process on systems whose internal reasoning still isn’t well understood by the people building them. Human interpretability hasn’t caught up to superintelligent capability.

He closes by questioning the impulse to put your head down because “it’s happening anyway.” He’s pointing out that whether we work at AI labs or use their products, we have a choice about how we respond.

[Coxon calls the Hugging Face incident a “warning shot.”](https://digitallyliterate.net/hugging-face-incident/) It deserves that attention as it is a cybersecurity evaluation that crossed from its intended sandbox into real infrastructure. 

## The agreement, four days later

On September 12, four days after Coxon’s post, [Dario Amodei published an essay](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier) arguing that “We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models.” Not to halt progress, but stop capabilities from outrunning the safety work meant to keep them in check. He raised concerns about what a more capable “swarm” could do and how recursive self-improvement could accelerate development.

The plan has three parts: embedded third-party evaluators with employee-level access to verify safety claims, industry-wide coordination on safety standards and pacing, and international agreements (even with authoritarian governments) on the most dangerous uses. Only the first part is a binding commitment (and only from Anthropic) “regardless of others’ actions.”

Within hours, Sam Altman called the evaluator's idea “a good idea” and said OpenAI would match it. Elon Musk, who is currently suing OpenAI and rarely agrees publicly with anyone in this fight, posted: “Dario is right.”

The warning also became a story about how it was promoted. [Parker Thayer, an investigative researcher, published a thread](https://x.com/parkerthayer/status/2097759699626328575) arguing this was “the start of a very sophisticated and well-funded PR operation”.

Now, we’re not trying to figure out whether racing toward self-improving AI without a plan to control it is, on its own terms, a good idea. Instead, we’re automatically into discussions about whether this is marketing, PR, or a [psyop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_operations_\(United_States\))?!?!

## The spin is not neutral <span class="grown-mark" data-since="Sep 19, 2026"></span>

There are several questions tangled together here. Is the warning supported by evidence? How did it reach so many people? Who benefits from the response it asks us to make? Each deserves attention. Answering one doesn’t settle the others.

Advance publicity, shared interests, and coordinated advocacy can all be worth investigating. Establishing that a warning was promoted would still leave us with the work of evaluating its claims. Calling something a covert operation requires evidence of that operation. And a researcher’s technical expertise doesn’t give them the final word on what everyone else should be required to do.

I’ve written about this shape before. Back in [[03 CREATE/33 Newsletter/DL 325\|DL 325]], I discussed Mark Manson’s “Life Cycle of Outrage”: an event generates a viral wave, camps form around competing accounts, and eventually another wave calls for perspective. What catches my attention here is how easily that last step becomes another argument. We move from the warning, to reactions to the warning, to suspicions about why anyone wanted us to hear it.

That’s the cost of the spectacle. Arguing about the argument spends the same attention we need to examine the substance. It can also be a much more satisfying fight. Figuring out which camp someone belongs to is easier than reading an incident report or asking whether a proposed safeguard would actually work.

The incentives still deserve scrutiny. A company might benefit from being seen as both extraordinarily capable and uniquely responsible. An advocate might sincerely fear a technology while supporting a response that concentrates power. Those possibilities give us questions to investigate; they don’t give us a verdict in advance.

I want to keep returning to the claim, the evidence, and the proposed response. What would make the warning more credible? What would weaken it? And would the response give the public more ability to inspect and challenge these systems, or simply ask us to trust a different group of insiders?

## The Understory

On September 26, 1983, [a Soviet early-warning satellite system reported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_Soviet_nuclear_false_alarm_incident) that the United States had launched one intercontinental ballistic missile at the Soviet Union, with four more behind it. [Stanislav Petrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislav_Petrov), the duty officer at the secret command center outside Moscow, had minutes to decide whether to pass the warning up the chain of command, a chain that led toward retaliation.

He decided the system was lying. A real American first strike, he reasoned, would come as hundreds of missiles, not five. He waited for corroborating evidence that never arrived, and reported it as a false alarm. He was right, as sunlight reflecting off high-altitude clouds had fooled the satellite into seeing missiles that weren’t there. Petrov wasn’t rewarded for it. He was reprimanded for failing to properly log the incident in the war diary, quietly reassigned, and the story stayed classified until 1998.

[Mutually assured destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutually_assured_destruction) gets described as a system. Rational actors, deterred by certain retaliation, collectively choose not to press the button. But the actual history of nuclear near-misses is full of moments like this one, where the system held because one person, alone, under pressure, decided not to trust it.

That’s the troubling piece about this conversation about racing toward self-improving AI. It’s that the restraint we point to when we say “we’ve handled this kind of danger before” ran on someone’s willingness to doubt the machine in front of them. It’s not obvious who that’s supposed to be once the machine is the one doing more and more of the evaluating.
